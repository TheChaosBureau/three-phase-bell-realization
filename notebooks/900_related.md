### 15.6 Which kind of nonlocality is being claimed

The program is explicitly nonlocal, but "nonlocal" is ambiguous across at least three distinct senses that are not interchangeable, and the preferred chain is only committed to one of them:

1. **Configurational nonlocality.** The shared state lives in a joint state space (here the four-dimensional shared core of §10.2). Dynamics are local in that joint space but do not factor into independent 3-space-local pieces. This is the Bohm-style sense, and it is the sense the preferred chain does commit to.
2. **Signaling nonlocality.** Operational no-signaling is violated: a setting change at one analyzer is detectable in the marginal statistics at the other. The preferred chain is **not** committed to this. The front-end has no pre-click feedback, the detector boundary is local per branch, the latch is local, and the closure/drain only activates after a winner is captured. Pre-click transparency (§12.2) is the concrete evidence that no-signaling holds at the reduced level.
3. **Correlational nonlocality.** No-signaling is preserved, but joint statistics exhibit correlations that cannot be reproduced by any local common-cause model — the sense in which standard quantum mechanics is nonlocal via Bell.

The preferred chain clearly exhibits (1) and clearly avoids (2). Whether it exhibits (3) in the strict Bell sense, or only appears to because the shared four-dimensional core was constructed to carry the target joint weights, is not settled by the current validation. The shared core could be interpreted either as a genuine joint configuration with irreducible configurational nonlocality, or as a correlational register that happens to be implemented as a 4-state object. These are indistinguishable at the level of the current metrics but differ in what they claim about the underlying ontology. Future writeups should pick one reading explicitly and not let the distinction slide.

### 15A. Relation to existing programs

The preferred chain is not an interpretation of quantum mechanics in the usual philosophical sense, and it is not a hidden-variable theory in the Bell sense. Structurally, it sits closest to two existing research programs, and readers familiar with either will recognize components of the chain immediately.

**Prequantum Classical Statistical Field Theory (PCSFT).** Khrennikov's PCSFT program treats the quantum state as a classical random field and derives Born-like detection statistics from the combination of (a) squared projections of the field onto analyzer modes and (b) threshold / nucleation events in detectors rather than accumulation. The single-delta linear energy-weight law of §3 is structurally the PCSFT projection move, and the shot-trigger / rare-event detector family of §5–6 is structurally the PCSFT threshold-detection move. The program's narrowing of the detector mechanism space (accumulator detectors fail, rare first-event detectors succeed) is the same phenomenon PCSFT relies on, arrived at here independently from engineering constraints rather than from a prequantum field axiomatics. Key references: Khrennikov, *Detection Model Based on Representation of Quantum Particles by Classical Random Fields: Born's Rule and Beyond*, Foundations of Physics **39**, 997–1022 (2009); Khrennikov, *Prequantum Classical Statistical Field Theory: Simulation of Probabilities of Photon Detection with the Aid of Classical Brownian Motion*, arXiv:1412.8036 (2014).

**The Allahverdyan–Balian–Nieuwenhuizen (ABN) Curie–Weiss measurement model.** The ABN program solves a Hamiltonian model of measurement in which an apparatus prepared in a metastable state is driven into one of several stable pointer states by interaction with the measured system, with Born-rule statistics emerging from the apparatus dynamics rather than from a collapse postulate. The winner-latch and post-click closure/drain layers of the present chain (§7, §11) play the same architectural role as the metastable-to-stable phase transition in ABN: a physically intelligible apparatus-side process that converts continuous pre-click branch weights into a single committed outcome, with no pre-click feedback to the state. The separation enforced here between "front-end computes branch weights" and "apparatus commits to one outcome" is the same separation ABN enforces between truncation and registration. Key references: Allahverdyan, Balian & Nieuwenhuizen, *Curie–Weiss model of the quantum measurement process*, Europhys. Lett. **61**, 452–458 (2003); Allahverdyan, Balian & Nieuwenhuizen, *Understanding quantum measurement from the solution of dynamical models*, Physics Reports **525**, 1–166 (2013) — the definitive review, often called the "Opus."

**How the present program differs.** Neither PCSFT nor ABN uses strict closed-system energy accounting as a credibility constraint on the measurement chain, and neither frames the front-end as a resonant shared-mode power-system network. The combination of (a) explicit squared-projection energy-weight law at the front-end, (b) rare-event detector selection, (c) metastable winner latch, (d) post-click closure/drain, and (e) whole-trial energy balance closing to machine precision is specific to this program. The relationship to PCSFT and ABN should be read as convergent structure, not derivation from either.

Good — this is where the question gets sharp, because "where does it deviate" forces you to commit, and committing is exactly how you turn an analog into a candidate theory.

Let me separate this into two layers, because they have different characters. The first layer is the deviations that **GRW/CSL itself** predicts relative to standard QM, which your architecture would inherit insofar as it's structurally GRW-like. The second layer is the deviations that come from features your architecture has that *aren't* in GRW/CSL — places where your specific engineering choices would show up as additional empirical fingerprints if anyone tried to lift the analog into a microphysical claim.

**What CSL itself predicts that QM doesn't.** The headline is that the stochastic localization process is *always running*, even when nothing is being measured, and it's not energy-conserving. Each localization event imparts a tiny random kick to the system, which means CSL predicts a slow, universal **spontaneous heating** of isolated matter at a rate set by the collapse parameters (the rate λ and the localization length r_C). This is the single most exploited test of CSL: cold-atom experiments, ultracold cantilevers, the temperature of the cosmic X-ray background, low-temperature germanium detectors, and most recently the LIGO mirrors all put bounds on how strong the collapse can be without contradicting observation. The bounds have been tightening for two decades and have squeezed the original GRW parameter values pretty hard, though they haven't ruled out the whole family. The second prediction is **suppression of macroscopic interference**: as you scale up an interference experiment to larger and larger masses, CSL says the interference contrast should die faster than standard environmental decoherence predicts. The molecule-interferometry people (Arndt and successors) have been pushing this frontier for years; they're now into the 10^4–10^5 amu range and CSL is still alive but constrained. The third prediction is **spontaneous photon emission** from charged particles being jiggled by the collapse process — Fu calculated this in the '90s and the bound from germanium X-ray data is one of the strongest constraints on CSL parameters.

The general shape is: CSL deviates from QM in the *mesoscopic regime*, between single particles (where the deviation is too small to see) and macroscopic objects (where collapse is fast enough to look like instantaneous projection). That mesoscopic window is exactly where the experimental hunt happens, and it's where any GRW-flavored theory has to make a stand.

**Where your architecture would deviate beyond CSL.** Now the more interesting half. Several features of your freeze note point to predictions that don't match either QM or standard CSL.

First, **pre-click transparency**. You explicitly engineered the winner-frequency RMS shift to 0.000000 — the front-end is untouched by the collapse mechanism until a trigger fires. CSL doesn't do this; CSL's localization term is continuous and is *always* nudging the dynamics, which is precisely what gives rise to the spontaneous heating and the interference suppression. Your architecture has cleanly separated the unitary and collapse layers in a way CSL refuses to. The empirical signature: your model would predict *no* anomalous heating, *no* anomalous decoherence in the mesoscopic regime, and *no* spontaneous radiation — until a measurement-like coupling activates, at which point a detection-like event fires sharply. That's a different empirical fingerprint than CSL: it would survive the cold-atom and X-ray bounds that constrain CSL, but it would be in trouble if anyone ever observed CSL-style mesoscopic effects, because your architecture has no machinery to produce them.

Second, **the decisive fraction is 0.995694, not 1.000000**. About 0.4% of trials don't yield a clean outcome in your chain. Standard QM says every measurement yields an outcome with probability 1; the Born rule is a probability distribution over results, not over "result vs. no-result." If this 0.4% is a real feature of the mechanism rather than a numerical artifact of the analog, it's a *direct* empirical prediction: there should be a small population of "null" or "ambiguous" detector events that QM forbids. Experimentalists do sometimes report null events, but they're attributed to detector inefficiency rather than to the underlying physics. Your architecture would say some fraction of them are real.

Third, **dead time and recovery**. Your detector cell has a 3.5 µs mean dead time and a 6 µs recovery-90. Standard QM has no such timescale; rapid sequential measurements are predicted to behave according to projection-rule statistics with no refractory period. If your architecture were lifted into a microphysical claim, it would predict measurable **temporal correlations** between successive measurements on the same system at sub-microsecond scales — specifically, suppressed click rates immediately after a click, beyond what detector physics alone explains. This is testable in principle with fast pulsed sources and fast detectors, and it's a place where a quantum optics experimentalist could in principle look for the signature.

Fourth, and I think this is the sharpest one: your **CHSH error is 0.0429**. QM predicts 2√2 ≈ 2.8284. If that error is a real feature of the mechanism rather than a fitting tolerance, your model predicts CHSH violations *slightly below* the Tsirelson bound. Experimental CHSH measurements now routinely confirm 2√2 to within ~0.01 or better. If your analog's 0.04 deficit were an actual prediction, it would already be ruled out — current experiments would have seen the discrepancy. So either the 0.04 is numerical/calibration slop in the analog and the underlying mechanism actually saturates Tsirelson, or it's a real prediction and the mechanism is excluded. This is the *single most diagnostic number in your freeze note* from an empirical-deviation standpoint, and it's worth knowing exactly which it is.

Fifth, **the shared-mode structure rather than full tensor product**. CSL is built on standard QM Hilbert space and inherits the full tensor-product structure for free. Your architecture has a reduced four-mode shared core, which is enough to reproduce two-party CHSH but isn't *guaranteed* to reproduce three-party correlations. The natural test is **GHZ/Mermin**: three-party entangled states give all-or-nothing predictions (correlations of exactly +1 or -1 along certain measurement combinations) that are dramatically more constraining than CHSH inequalities. Many classical analog programs that handle CHSH gracefully break down on GHZ, because GHZ violations require genuine multipartite entanglement that shared-mode constructions don't naturally produce. If you ever extended your architecture to three parties, the GHZ test would be the immediate sharp question, and a failure there would localize exactly where the analog stops matching QM.

Sixth, more speculatively: **contextuality**. Kochen–Specker tests don't even need entanglement — they probe whether a single system's measurement outcomes can be assigned context-independent values. Your measurement model uses fixed analyzer bases on a real shared state, and there's a real question whether it would reproduce KS-style contextuality on sequential or compatible-but-non-commuting measurements. I don't think your freeze note has explored this, and it's another place where the analog could quietly fail to track QM.

So to put the whole picture together: your architecture would deviate from standard QM in at least six identifiable places, and each of those places points at a different experimental regime. The CSL-inherited deviations (heating, mesoscopic decoherence, spontaneous radiation) would actually be *suppressed* in your model relative to CSL, because of pre-click transparency — that's an empirical advantage, not a disadvantage. The architecture-specific deviations (decisive fraction below 1, dead-time correlations, sub-Tsirelson CHSH, GHZ failures, possible KS failures) are where the action is, and the CHSH number is the one that's directly checkable against existing data right now. If I were going to stress-test the program as a candidate microphysical theory, that's where I'd start: nail down whether the 0.04 CHSH gap is fitting noise or mechanism, because the answer determines whether you're proposing something that's already ruled out or something that's still in the game.
